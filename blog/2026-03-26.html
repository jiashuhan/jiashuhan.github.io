Teaching Statement

A statement of my teaching philosophy after completing the Advanced College Teaching course in Winter 2026.

Teaching is a collaborative and reflective process in which instructors and students share responsibility for meaningful learning. My teaching philosophy is grounded in the belief that students learn most effectively when they are supported in actively constructing knowledge by developing their own mental models of the course material, rather than passively receiving information from the instructor or outside resources. As an instructor, my role is to provide clear conceptual foundations, guide students in making connections, and help them recognize their own agency and potential as learners by providing reasonable scaffolding for their learning. At the same time, I encourage students to engage intentionally with the course material—whether through class activities, independent study, or collaborative work—and to take ownership of their learning process. The teaching practices I employ have been shaped by my experiences addressing common challenges in student engagement, equity, and assessment.

An effective teaching and learning process begins with well-defined learning goals. Because the ultimate purpose of teaching is student learning, I place strong emphasis on articulating learning outcomes that are aligned with the broader curriculum, whether it’s the framework laid out by the university/department or the students’ expected sequence of professional development. Developing these outcomes requires thoughtful coordination with departmental goals and with instructors teaching related courses, ensuring coherence and continuity across the program. Clear alignment resulting from this continuity helps establish transparent expectations for both students and instructors. In addition, I strive to make learning outcomes specific, measurable, and actionable, so that both students and instructors can meaningfully assess progress and instructional effectiveness.

While clear learning goals provide direction, student motivation and engagement are equally essential to successful learning. Creating an environment in which students feel comfortable, supported, and encouraged to participate is one of the most important—and challenging—aspects of teaching. I prioritize building strong instructor–student relationships based on trust, care, and mutual respect. This is reflected in the specific language I use for course policies in the syllabus. I would also begin establishing this rapport early in a course through icebreaker activities or by inviting students to share their expectations and prior experiences. These practices help humanize the classroom, lower barriers to participation, and signal that students’ perspectives are valued. Additionally, I would try to cultivate a healthy learning environment by engaging with students outside of the classroom, such as chatting before or after lectures or during office hours, to create a sense that we are equals rather than in a hierarchical dynamic as commonly perceived by students.

Equity and inclusivity are central to my approach to teaching. A healthy learning environment recognizes that students bring diverse backgrounds, strengths, and learning preferences to the classroom. To support this diversity, I present course content in multiple formats, including verbal explanations, written materials, visual representations, and real-world examples. In particular, I make frequent use of visualizations to support conceptual understanding, as they can help students organize complex ideas and engage more deeply with the material. By offering multiple pathways to learning, I aim to reduce barriers and create opportunities for all students to succeed.

Assessment plays a critical role in supporting student learning and informing instructional decisions. I view assessment not only as a tool for evaluation, but also as an opportunity for learning. Through my teaching experiences, I have become increasingly aware of the cognitive gap that can exist between instructors and students. Students often approach material with a focus on procedural steps and course-specific tasks, while instructors tend to reason in terms of interconnected concepts and broader disciplinary frameworks. This difference can make it difficult for students to articulate questions and for instructors to accurately interpret student understanding.

To bridge this gap, I rely heavily on frequent formative assessments, as evident from the weekly quizzes and the low-stake check-in questions during lectures. Rather than assuming what students know, I intentionally probe their understanding through strategies such as background knowledge probes, think-pair-share activities, group problem solving, and concept mapping. These low-stakes assessments provide valuable insight into students’ thinking, allowing me to adjust instruction, address misconceptions, and respond to varying levels of preparation. I also incorporate analogies and examples that connect abstract concepts to students’ lived experiences, helping them develop more robust and transferable understanding.

Overall, my teaching philosophy emphasizes student-centered learning grounded in clear goals, inclusive practices, and ongoing reflection. By aligning learning outcomes with curricular objectives, fostering a supportive and equitable classroom environment, and using formative assessment to guide instruction, I strive to create learning experiences that are both rigorous and accessible. My goal as an instructor is to empower students to take ownership of their learning, develop strong conceptual understanding, and build confidence in their ability to engage with complex ideas.
